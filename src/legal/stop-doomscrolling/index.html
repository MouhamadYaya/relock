How to stop doomscrolling on your iPhone

Nobody decides to spend an hour scrolling. The hour happens because the feed has no ending and the evening has no edge. This page is about where to put an edge — before you open the app, and once you are already inside it.

Written for iPhone Habit, not diagnosis Practical steps first

Short answer

Decide the ending before you start. Open the feed with a reason and a stopping point — "ten minutes, then I make dinner" — because the app will never offer one. Then remove the two easiest entrances: notifications from that app, and its position on your first home screen.

For the evenings when that is not enough, put something between the tap and the feed: a schedule that closes the app after a certain hour, or a delay that makes you notice you are reaching for it.

1. What the word actually means

In everyday use, doomscrolling means continuing to scroll through distressing or agitating content long after it has stopped being useful — usually news or comment feeds, usually late, usually without having chosen to start.

It is a description of a behaviour, not a medical condition. There is no diagnosis called doomscrolling, and nothing on this page is medical advice. If you are reading it because you feel genuinely unwell, the last section says the only honest thing there is to say about that.

The distinction matters practically, not just semantically. A habit responds to changed conditions — different cues, different paths, different endings. Framing it as a personal failing produces guilt, and guilt has never closed an app.

2. Why the feed never gives you a stopping point

A book has chapters. A television episode has credits. A newspaper has a last page. Every medium that existed before the feed had a built-in moment where continuing required a fresh decision, and you made a lot of those decisions in favour of stopping.

An infinite feed removes that moment by design. Content loads before you reach the bottom, so the bottom never arrives. Video plays automatically, so the gap where you might have looked up never opens. And what comes next is unpredictable — mostly forgettable, occasionally excellent — which is exactly the pattern that keeps someone going one more time.

Two consequences follow, and they are the whole basis of anything that works:

  • If you do not bring an ending, there will not be one. Willpower is being asked to supply something the medium deliberately withholds, continuously, for as long as you are in it.
  • The cheapest place to intervene is before you open the app — because once you are inside, you are arguing with a system built to keep the argument going.

3. The three moments it starts

Watch yourself for a few days and it is almost always one of these. Knowing which one is yours saves you from applying the wrong fix.

In bed

The phone is the last thing in the room

Nothing else is available, you are tired, and tired is when the reaching is most automatic. Fixed by changing the room, not the mind.

Dead time

Ninety seconds with nothing to do

Queues, lifts, waiting rooms. The scroll is a reflex against a small gap — and it routinely outlives the gap by twenty minutes.

Avoidance

Something else is waiting

The hardest one, because the feed is not the problem — it is the exit. Blocking it alone tends to relocate the avoidance rather than end it.

4. Put the edge before the app

  • Open with a reason and a stop. Say it before you tap: what you are going in for, and what happens when you come out. An ending you brought is the only kind you will get.
  • Turn its notifications off entirely. Not just badges. An app that can summon you does not need you to remember it exists.
  • Take it off the first home screen. Reaching by search takes one extra deliberate second — and that second is often enough to notice you were not deciding anything.
  • Give the last hour of the day to something else. Anything with an ending. A chapter, an episode, a walk. The point is the ending, not the virtue.
  • Charge the phone in another room. The most effective single change most people make, and it needs no app at all.

5. Getting out once you are already scrolling

Different problem, different tools. Mid-scroll you are not making decisions well, so the technique has to be small enough to work while distracted.

  • Say what you are looking for, out loud. Usually the honest answer is "nothing", and hearing yourself say it does more than another resolution would.
  • Change your posture. Sit up, stand, put both feet on the floor. The body is often holding the loop in place more than the mind is.
  • Set a real alarm, not a mental one. "Five more minutes" without a timer means twenty-five.
  • Do not aim for the perfect exit. Closing the app one screen after you noticed is a win. Waiting for a natural stopping point means waiting forever, on purpose.

6. Where a pause fits

Everything above is free and needs no software. What software can add is a reliable interruption at the exact instant your hand moves — the moment you cannot supervise, because you are not present for it.

That is what Relock is for. When a protected app opens, an iPhone app blocker built on Apple Screen Time puts a screen in front of it that asks what you are actually looking for, and counts down before letting you through. At the end you can still choose Open anyway — deliberately, because the aim is to convert an automatic gesture into a decision, not to win an argument with you.

For evenings specifically, two rule shapes tend to fit: a time window that closes the feeds from a set hour, and a cap on how many times an app can open in a day. If you would rather start with the free tools, the guide to reducing screen time on iPhone covers those first.

7. When it is not just a habit

Sometimes scrolling is the symptom rather than the cause. If the evenings are hard for reasons that have nothing to do with the phone, blocking an app relocates the difficulty without touching it — and a tool that promises otherwise is selling something.

If low mood, anxiety or sleep problems persist, that is worth raising with a doctor or a qualified professional. Relock is a focus-support tool. It does not treat, diagnose or manage any medical or mental-health condition, and it should not be used as a substitute for care.

8. Frequently asked questions

What is doomscrolling?

In everyday use it means continuing to scroll through distressing or agitating content long after it has stopped being useful — typically news or comment feeds, typically late at night, and typically without having decided to start. It describes a behaviour; it is not a medical diagnosis.

Why is it so hard to stop scrolling?

Because the feed removes the moment where you would have decided. Content loads before you reach the bottom, video plays on its own, and what comes next is unpredictable enough to be worth one more look. Nothing in the design ever asks whether you want to continue, so the only ending is one you bring yourself.

Does blocking social media apps actually stop doomscrolling?

It reliably stops the reflex opening, which is where most of an evening goes. It does not address the case where scrolling is a way of avoiding something else — there, the app is the exit rather than the cause, and blocking it alone tends to move the avoidance somewhere new.

Should I delete social media apps instead?

For a small number of apps, yes. For most people deletion fails within a fortnight because the app is genuinely useful sometimes. Rules are for the apps you want to keep without being pulled into them.

Can Relock help me stop doomscrolling at night?

Yes, in one specific way: a time-window rule makes the apps you chose unavailable during hours you set, such as 10pm–8am, and a progressive delay puts a growing countdown in front of an app you keep reaching for. It is not a treatment for anything, and it keeps an emergency unlock so it never holds your phone hostage.
