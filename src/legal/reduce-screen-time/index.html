How to reduce screen time on iPhone

Most screen-time advice assumes the problem is a lack of discipline. It usually is not. Below is what actually moves the number, in the order worth doing it — starting with the free tools already on the phone, and ending with what to do when those stop being enough.

Short answer

Open Settings → Screen Time and read one week of real data before changing anything. Then turn off notifications for every app that is not a person, move the two or three apps you open by reflex off your first home screen, and set Downtime plus one App Limit on the app that takes the most from you.

That is enough for most people. If you find yourself tapping past Apple's limit every day, the problem is not the limit — it is that dismissing it costs nothing. That is the point where friction, or an app blocker, starts to earn its place.

1. Look at the real number before you change anything

Settings → Screen Time already holds a week of honest data: daily average, the split by app, and — the useful one almost nobody scrolls down to — Pickups, the number of times you picked the phone up, and which app you opened first.

Read the pickups number first. Someone at four hours a day across eighty pickups has a very different problem from someone at four hours across twelve. The first is a habit of reaching; the second is a habit of staying. They need opposite fixes, and starting with the wrong one is why so many attempts fail in week two.

Resist the urge to set a goal on day one. A number chosen before you know your own pattern is a number you will miss on Wednesday and abandon on Thursday. Give it seven days of just looking.

2. Stop being summoned

A large share of screen time is not chosen at all — it is answered. Every banner is an invitation, and you accept most of them without noticing.

The rule that survives: a notification is allowed only if a person is on the other end of it. Messages, calls, calendar. Everything else — feeds, suggestions, "someone you may know", re-engagement nudges written by a growth team — goes off. In Settings → Notifications, work down the list once. It takes ten minutes and it is the highest-return ten minutes in this guide.

Keep badges off too, especially the red dot. A number on an icon is a small unfinished task, and unfinished tasks are exactly what your attention is built to chase.

3. Move the triggers, not just the apps

An automatic opening needs three things: a cue, an easy path, and a reward. You cannot remove the reward, and you rarely control the cue. The path is yours.

  • Clear the first home screen. Leave nothing on it that you open by reflex. The apps still exist — you reach them through search, which takes an extra deliberate second.
  • Break the muscle memory. Your thumb knows a position, not an icon. Moving an app one screen over is enough to make you notice you were reaching at all.
  • Charge the phone outside the bedroom. The single most effective change most people make, and it requires no app and no settings.
  • Log out of the worst one. Not delete — log out. Re-entering a password is small friction, but it is friction that arrives before the feed does.

4. Use Apple's own limits, honestly

Screen Time is free, already installed, and enough for a lot of people. Set it up properly before paying for anything.

Downtime

Hours when only what you allow can open

The closest thing iOS has to a schedule. Best used on sleep — set it to begin before you want to be asleep, not when you want to be asleep.

App Limits

A daily time budget per app or category

Useful for the one app that dominates your report. Less useful applied to six at once, because you will start dismissing them all.

Always Allowed

What stays reachable during Downtime

Keep it short and boring: phone, messages, maps. Everything you add here is a door you have left open.

Focus modes

Filter what reaches you, by context

Separate from Screen Time, and complementary: Screen Time governs what you can open, Focus governs what interrupts you.

Two honest caveats. First, App Limits count minutes, which measures staying rather than reaching — if your problem is thirty quick checks a day, a time budget barely touches it. Second, when the limit arrives, a single tap gets past it. For a deliberate override that is correct behaviour. For a reflex, an override that costs one tap is not a limit at all.

5. Make app-free windows instead of app-free days

Full-day detoxes are satisfying to announce and almost never repeated. Windows work better because they are small enough to keep on a bad day, and they attach to something you already do.

Three that tend to survive:

  • The first thirty minutes after waking. Whatever you open first tends to set the tempo of the morning.
  • One protected work block. Not the whole day. Ninety minutes you were going to work anyway.
  • The last hour before bed. The one where "five more minutes" reliably becomes an hour.

Pick one. Adding all three at once is the same mistake as a full-day detox, spread thinner.

6. When the limits stop working

There is a specific failure everyone recognises: the limit appears, you dismiss it without reading it, and the app opens. It happened so fast you could not describe the decision afterwards, because there was not one.

At that point more restriction is not the answer — the restriction is already there. What is missing is time between the gesture and the app. That is what a friction-based iPhone app blocker adds, and it is a different mechanism from a time budget:

  • A delay that grows. The first opening of the afternoon costs almost nothing. The fifth costs enough that you notice you are on the fifth. In Relock this is the progressive delay.
  • A count, not a clock. A cap on how many times an app may open per day measures reaching rather than staying — the thing App Limits cannot see.
  • A commitment you cannot immediately undo. Strict mode makes a rule unstoppable before it ends, so the decision belongs to the calm version of you rather than the tired one.

Why a delay beats a wall goes into the reasoning, including why Relock keeps an Open anyway button at the end of the countdown on purpose.

7. A first week that actually holds

  • Days 1–7: change nothing. Read Screen Time once, including pickups.
  • Day 8: notifications. Only people get through.
  • Day 9: clear the first home screen.
  • Day 10: Downtime on the sleep hours, and one App Limit on the app at the top of your report.
  • Day 17: read the report again. If you are dismissing the limit daily, add friction rather than more limits.

Expect the number to move by a useful amount, not a dramatic one, and expect a bad week somewhere in the first month. A rule you resume is worth more than a rule you never broke.

8. Frequently asked questions

What is a reasonable amount of screen time per day?

There is no threshold that is right for everyone, and any single number you see quoted is closer to marketing than to evidence. A more useful question than "how many hours" is "how much of it did I choose". Someone who spends three deliberate hours reading has a different situation from someone who spends two hours across sixty unplanned pickups.

Does Apple Screen Time actually work?

For deliberate use, yes — Downtime and App Limits are genuinely effective and they cost nothing. Their weak point is the reflex: limits are measured in minutes rather than openings, and a single tap gets past them, which is fine for a considered override and useless against an automatic one.

Should I just delete the app instead?

Deleting works, and for a small number of apps it is the right answer. For most people it fails because the app is genuinely useful sometimes — it comes back within a fortnight, usually on a bad evening. Rules exist for the apps you want to keep but not to be pulled into.

Do I need an app blocker to reduce my screen time?

No. Notifications, home-screen layout and Apple's built-in Screen Time cover most of the distance, for free. A blocker earns its place in one specific case: when you are already dismissing the limit every day without thinking about it.

Does greyscale help?

Some people find it takes the shine off a feed, and it costs nothing to try — Settings → Accessibility → Display & Text Size → Color Filters. Treat it as a small nudge rather than a fix; it removes some of the appeal but none of the ease of opening.
